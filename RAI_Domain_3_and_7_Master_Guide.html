Complete exam-focused breakdown covering AI governance frameworks, mathematical fairness definitions, bias mitigation pipelines, SHAP/LIME interpretability, adversarial robustness, model calibration, and data drift.

Domain 3: RAI — Governance and Ethical Fundamentals

Sub-topics: Governance, Ethics, Responsible AI

1. The 6 Core Pillars of Responsible AI

Assessments frequently test your ability to map real-world AI failures to the specific ethical pillar they violate:

1. Fairness & Inclusiveness

Ensuring AI systems treat all demographic groups equitably without encoding historical discrimination or disparate performance across protected attributes ($A$).

2. Reliability & Safety

Ensuring the system performs consistently under expected conditions, degrades gracefully on edge cases, and resists adversarial manipulation.

3. Privacy & Security

Protecting training data and user inputs against leakage, membership inference, model inversion, and unauthorized data extraction.

4. Transparency & Explainability

Providing visibility into how data was sourced, what model assumptions exist, and why specific predictions were made (intelligible to stakeholders).

5. Accountability & Governance

Establishing clear human ownership, audit trails, risk assessments, red-teaming sign-offs, and remediation mechanisms when harms occur.

6. Human-in-the-Loop (HITL)

Ensuring meaningful human oversight for high-stakes decisions (healthcare, credit, criminal justice) rather than rubber-stamping automated outputs.

2. Complete Taxonomy of AI Bias (Where Bias Enters the Pipeline)

A classic exam question gives a scenario and asks you to identify the exact type of bias at play:

Bias Type Pipeline Stage Definition & High-Reward Exam Trigger Classic Example
Historical Bias World / Pre-Data Real-world socio-historical inequalities already exist even if data sampling and measurement are 100% perfect. Resume screener trained on decades of male-dominated tech hiring.
Representation (Sampling) Bias Data Collection Certain subgroups are under-represented in the training distribution relative to the deployment population. Facial recognition trained predominantly on lighter skin tones failing on darker skin tones.
Measurement (Label) Bias Feature / Target Labeling The chosen proxy variable ($Y_{\text{proxy}}$) is a noisy or systematically skewed approximation of the true construct ($Y_{\text{true}}$). Using "healthcare cost spent" as a proxy for "illness severity" (undermines patients with lower healthcare access).
Aggregation Bias Model Architecture Using a single "one-size-fits-all" model across distinct populations where conditional feature-target relationships differ. Diabetes HbA1c thresholds functioning differently across ethnic groups when pooled into one model.
Evaluation Bias Validation / Testing Benchmark datasets do not reflect real-world target demographics, masking subgroup failures during testing. Evaluating an ASR speech model solely on standard American English benchmarks.
Deployment / Automation Bias Post-Deployment Humans over-trust automated recommendations (Automation Bias) or use the model outside its intended scope. Doctors accepting an AI sepsis alert without verifying contradictory clinical signs.

3. Mathematical Fairness Definitions (High-Yield Exam Formulas)

Let $A \in \{0, 1\}$ be a protected attribute (e.g., gender/race), $Y \in \{0, 1\}$ be the ground-truth label, and $\hat{Y} \in \{0, 1\}$ be the model's predicted outcome.

Fairness Metric Mathematical Formulation Core Intuition & Use Case
Demographic (Statistical) Parity $P(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid A = 0) = P(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid A = 1)$ Selection rate is equal across groups, regardless of ground-truth qualification $Y$. Used when historical labels $Y$ are deeply tainted.
Disparate Impact Ratio (80% Rule) $\frac{P(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid A = \text{unprivileged})}{P(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid A = \text{privileged})} \ge 0.80$ Legal/compliance standard (EEOC Four-Fifths Rule). A ratio below $0.8$ flags adverse impact in hiring or credit scoring.
Equal Opportunity $P(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid Y = 1, A = 0) = P(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid Y = 1, A = 1)$ True Positive Rates (TPR / Recall) are equal across groups. Qualified individuals have an equal chance of selection.
Equalized Odds $\text{TPR}_{A=0} = \text{TPR}_{A=1} \quad \text{AND} \quad \text{FPR}_{A=0} = \text{FPR}_{A=1}$ Stricter than Equal Opportunity: requires both equal True Positive Rates AND equal False Positive Rates across groups.
Predictive Parity (Calibration) $P(Y = 1 \mid \hat{Y} = 1, A = 0) = P(Y = 1 \mid \hat{Y} = 1, A = 1)$ Precision (PPV) is equal across groups. A positive prediction means the same likelihood of actual success regardless of group.
Individual Fairness $D(\hat{f}(x_i), \hat{f}(x_j)) \le L \cdot d(x_i, x_j)$ "Similar individuals receive similar predictions" (Lipschitz continuity condition across a task-specific similarity metric $d$).
⚠️ The Impossibility Theorem of Fairness (Chouldechova / Kleinberg)

Whenever base rates differ between groups ($P(Y=1 \mid A=0) \neq P(Y=1 \mid A=1)$) and a classifier is not 100% perfect, it is mathematically impossible to simultaneously satisfy Demographic Parity, Equalized Odds, and Predictive Parity (Calibration). Practitioners must choose which metric aligns with the domain's ethical and legal priority (e.g., COMPAS recidivism controversy: Northpointe optimized for Predictive Parity, resulting in higher False Positive Rates for Black defendants—violating Equalized Odds).

4. Where & How to Mitigate Bias (Pre-, In-, and Post-Processing)

1. Pre-Processing (Data Level)

Applied before model training when you can modify the dataset:

  • Reweighing: Assigns sample weights to $(A, Y)$ combinations to equalize statistical parity without altering feature values.
  • Disparate Impact Remover: Edits feature distributions to remove correlation with protected attribute $A$.
  • Targeted Oversampling / SMOTE: Balances underrepresented subgroup-label intersections.

2. In-Processing (Training Level)

Modifies the loss function or optimization constraints during model training:

  • Adversarial Debiasing: Trains a predictor to maximize task accuracy while an adversary network tries to predict protected attribute $A$ from the predictor's latent representations.
  • Fairness Regularization: Adds a penalty term $\lambda \cdot \mathcal{L}_{\text{fairness}}$ directly to the empirical risk loss function.

3. Post-Processing (Output Level)

Applied to predicted probabilities after training (ideal for black-box or frozen third-party models):

  • Group-Specific Thresholding: Sets distinct classification cutoffs ($\tau_0 \neq \tau_1$) per group to satisfy Equal Opportunity or Equalized Odds.
  • Reject Option Classification: Flips predictions near the decision boundary ($0.5 \pm \delta$) in favor of the unprivileged group.
  • Platt Scaling / Calibration: Adjusts probability outputs per subgroup.
🔥 Exam Trap: Why "Fairness Through Unawareness" Fails

Simply dropping the protected column (e.g., removing Race or Gender) is called Fairness Through Unawareness. It almost always fails because redundant encodings (proxy variables)—such as Zip Code, Browsing History, or Alma Mater—allow the model to reconstruct the protected attribute implicitly.

5. Major Regulatory & Governance Frameworks (NIST AI RMF & EU AI Act)

NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0)

Voluntary US framework structured around 4 Core Functions:

  1. GOVERN (Cross-cutting): Establish organizational risk culture, accountability roles, policies, and oversight boards.
  2. MAP: Contextualize the AI use case, categorize system risks, and identify downstream stakeholder impacts before building.
  3. MEASURE: Quantitatively and qualitatively test, benchmark, red-team, and evaluate fairness, drift, and robustness metrics.
  4. MANAGE: Prioritize identified risks, execute mitigation plans, monitor post-deployment incidents, and trigger fallbacks/decommissioning.

The EU AI Act (Risk-Based Tier System)

Binding regulation classifying AI by intended use case risk:

  1. Unacceptable Risk (Banned): Government social scoring, untargeted scraping for facial databases, subliminal manipulation, emotion recognition in workplaces/schools.
  2. High Risk (Strict Compliance): Credit scoring, recruitment/HR, critical infrastructure, medical devices, law enforcement, biometric ID. Requires conformity assessments, human oversight, data governance, and logging.
  3. Limited Risk (Transparency Duty): Chatbots, deepfakes, synthetic audio/video—must disclose that content is AI-generated.
  4. Minimal Risk (Unregulated): Spam filters, video game AI, inventory recommenders.

6. Privacy-Preserving AI & Transparency Documentation

  • Differential Privacy ($\epsilon, \delta$): Guarantees that whether any single individual's record is included or excluded from the dataset changes the probability of any model output by at most $e^\epsilon$. Achieved in deep learning via DP-SGD (clipping per-sample gradients to bound sensitivity + adding calibrated Gaussian noise). Smaller $\epsilon$ = stronger privacy, lower utility.
  • Federated Learning: Decentralized training where raw user data never leaves edge devices; only local weight updates/gradients are aggregated on a central server (often combined with Secure Aggregation).
  • Model Cards (Mitchell et al.): Standardized documentation attached to trained models detailing intended use cases, out-of-scope uses, disaggregated subgroup performance metrics, and ethical limitations.
  • Datasheets for Datasets (Gebru et al.): Standardized documentation detailing dataset motivation, composition, collection methodology, preprocessing, labeling rules, and known representation gaps.

Domain 7: RAI — Technical Reliability and Explainability

Sub-topics: Reliability, Robustness, Explainability, Model Behaviour

1. Taxonomy of Explainable AI (XAI)

Understand the three core axes used to classify interpretability methods on technical exams:

Intrinsic vs. Post-Hoc

Intrinsic (Glass-box): Interpretable by design due to simple structure (Linear/Logistic Regression, shallow Decision Trees, GAMs, Rule Lists).

Post-Hoc: Applied after training to explain complex black-box models (Deep Neural Nets, XGBoost, Random Forests) via SHAP, LIME, or Grad-CAM.

Global vs. Local

Global: Explains overall model behavior across the entire dataset distribution (Permutation Feature Importance, PDPs, Global SHAP summary).

Local: Explains why the model made a single specific prediction $\hat{f}(x_i)$ for an individual user (LIME, Local SHAP force plot, Counterfactuals).

Model-Agnostic vs. Model-Specific

Model-Agnostic: Treats the model as a black-box input-output function $\hat{f}(x)$; works on any architecture (LIME, KernelSHAP, PDP).

Model-Specific: Leverages internal structure such as gradients or tree splits (Integrated Gradients, Grad-CAM, TreeSHAP).

2. Deep Dive: LIME vs. SHAP & Shapley Value Mathematics

Questions comparing LIME and SHAP are the single highest-probability topic in Domain 7.

LIME (Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations)

How it works: Approximates a complex black-box model $f$ locally around a single instance $x$ by perturbing $x$ into synthetic neighbor samples $z'$, weighting them by proximity $\pi_x(z)$, and fitting a sparse linear surrogate model $g \in G$:

$$\xi(x) = \arg\min_{g \in G} \; \mathcal{L}(f, g, \pi_x) + \Omega(g)$$
  • Pros: Fast, works on tabular, text, and images (superpixels).
  • Cons (Exam Alert): Instability—random perturbation sampling can produce different explanations for the exact same instance $x$ on repeated runs; perturbations can also land in unrealistic out-of-distribution regions.

SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations)

How it works: Grounded in cooperative game theory. Treats features as "players" in a coalition working together to shift the prediction from the baseline expected value $E[f(X)]$ to the final prediction $f(x)$:

$$\phi_i(f, x) = \sum_{S \subseteq F \setminus \{i\}} \frac{|S|!\,(|F| - |S| - 1)!}{|F|!} \left[ f_x(S \cup \{i\}) - f_x(S) \right]$$
  • Additivity Property (Exam Favorite): The sum of all feature SHAP values for instance $x$ plus the base value equals the exact model prediction: $$f(x) = \phi_0 + \sum_{i=1}^{M} \phi_i$$
  • Variants: KernelSHAP (model-agnostic, slow $O(2^M)$ approximation) vs. TreeSHAP (exact, fast polynomial-time specifically for Tree/GBM ensembles).
📐 The 4 Theoretical Axioms Satisfied ONLY by SHAP (Not LIME)
  1. Local Accuracy (Efficiency): Feature attributions sum exactly to $f(x) - E[f(X)]$.
  2. Missingness: A feature with zero impact on the coalition gets $\phi_i = 0$.
  3. Consistency (Monotonicity): If a model changes so that a feature's marginal contribution increases or stays the same regardless of other features, its attribution $\phi_i$ will never decrease.
  4. Symmetry: Two features that contribute identically to all coalitions receive identical SHAP values.

3. Feature Effect Methods, Counterfactuals & Deep Learning XAI

Method Scope How It Works & Key Limitation (Exam Trap)
Permutation Feature Importance (PFI) Global, Agnostic Randomly shuffles one feature column in the validation set and measures how much model error increases. Trap: Fails when features are correlated (creates unrealistic combinations and splits importance across colinear features).
Partial Dependence Plot (PDP) Global, Agnostic Plots the marginal effect of 1 or 2 features on the average predicted outcome across the dataset. Trap: Assumes feature independence and hides heterogeneous interaction effects (e.g., if half the instances go +10 and half go -10, the PDP line looks flat at 0).
Individual Conditional Expectation (ICE) Local/Global, Agnostic Plots one curve per individual instance showing how that specific instance's prediction changes as feature $x_S$ varies. Fixes PDP's flaw: Reveals heterogeneous subgroup interactions (PDP is simply the average of all ICE curves).
Accumulated Local Effects (ALE) Global, Agnostic Averages differences in predictions over small conditional windows rather than marginalizing over the whole distribution. Fixes PDP's flaw when features are strongly correlated.
Counterfactual Explanations Local, Agnostic Finds the smallest actionable change $\Delta x$ to input $x$ that flips the prediction to a desired target $y'$ (e.g., "If your annual income were \$5,000 higher, your loan would be approved"). Must optimize for proximity, sparsity, and feasibility.
Integrated Gradients (IG) Local, Deep Nets Accumulates gradients along a straight-line path from a neutral baseline $x'$ (e.g., black image or zero embedding) to input $x$: $\text{IG}_i(x) = (x_i - x'_i) \times \int_0^1 \frac{\partial F(x' + \alpha(x - x'))}{\partial x_i} d\alpha$. Solves gradient saturation in vanilla saliency maps.
Grad-CAM Local, CNN Vision Uses gradients of the target class score flowing into the final convolutional layer to produce a coarse spatial localization heatmap highlighting which image regions drove the prediction.

4. Technical Robustness & Adversarial Machine Learning

Robustness measures whether a model maintains stable, accurate predictions under small worst-case perturbations ($\|\delta\|_p \le \epsilon$), noisy inputs, or distributional shifts.

Attack Threat Taxonomy

  • Evasion Attack (Inference-Time): Adding imperceptible adversarial noise $\delta$ to test input $x$ to cause misclassification ($f(x + \delta) \neq y$).
  • Data Poisoning / Backdoor (Training-Time): Injecting corrupted samples or a hidden trigger pattern (e.g., a 3x3 pixel patch) into training data so the model behaves normally until the trigger appears.
  • White-Box vs. Black-Box: White-box attackers have full access to model weights $\theta$ and gradients $\nabla_x J$; Black-box attackers only query API inputs/outputs (relying on transferability from a surrogate model).

Core Gradient-Based Evasion Attacks

  • FGSM (Fast Gradient Sign Method — Goodfellow): Single-step attack nudging input $x$ in the direction of the loss gradient sign with respect to the input: $$x_{\text{adv}} = x + \epsilon \cdot \text{sign}\left(\nabla_x J(\theta, x, y)\right)$$
  • PGD (Projected Gradient Descent — Madry): Iterative multi-step variant of FGSM that takes smaller steps $\alpha$ and projects back onto the $\epsilon$-ball $\mathcal{B}_\epsilon(x)$: $$x^{t+1} = \Pi_{x + \mathcal{S}} \left( x^t + \alpha \cdot \text{sign}(\nabla_x J(\theta, x^t, y)) \right)$$

How to Defend Models (Adversarial Robustness Strategies)

  • Adversarial Training (Min-Max Optimization): The gold-standard empirical defense. Generates adversarial examples on the fly during training and minimizes the worst-case loss: $$\min_{\theta} \; \mathbb{E}_{(x,y) \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[ \max_{\|\delta\|_p \le \epsilon} J(\theta, x + \delta, y) \right]$$
  • Certified Robustness (Randomized Smoothing): Adds isotropic Gaussian noise $\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I)$ to the input across multiple forward passes and takes the majority vote, providing a mathematically provable $\ell_2$ radius within which no adversarial perturbation can flip the class.

5. Production Reliability: Distribution Drift, Calibration & Uncertainty

Understanding why models degrade silently in production is a staple of Domain 7 technical reliability questions:

Drift Type Mathematical Change What Changed in the Real World? Detection Metrics
Covariate Shift (Data Drift) $P_{\text{train}}(X) \neq P_{\text{prod}}(X)$, while $P(Y \mid X)$ stays constant Input feature distribution shifted (e.g., sudden influx of younger loan applicants), but the underlying rule linking features to default hasn't changed. KS-Test (continuous 1D), Population Stability Index (PSI), Wasserstein Distance, Chi-Square (categorical).
Prior Probability (Label) Shift $P_{\text{train}}(Y) \neq P_{\text{prod}}(Y)$, while $P(X \mid Y)$ stays constant The base rate of the target class shifted (e.g., sudden spike in flu prevalence during winter). Monitoring predicted class distribution shifts or ground-truth label rates.
Concept Drift $P_{\text{train}}(Y \mid X) \neq P_{\text{prod}}(Y \mid X)$ The fundamental relationship between inputs and target changed (e.g., COVID-19 lockdown changing consumer spending behavior or fraudsters inventing new attack patterns). Requires retraining. Monitoring rolling F1/AUC decay, residual errors, or ADWIN / Page-Hinkley drift detectors.

Model Calibration (Can You Trust $\hat{p}$?)

Modern deep neural networks are notoriously overconfident (producing $0.99$ softmax probabilities even when wrong). A model is well-calibrated if among all samples given probability $\hat{p} = 0.8$, roughly $80\%$ actually belong to the positive class:

$$P(Y = 1 \mid \hat{P} = p) = p$$
  • Evaluation: Reliability Diagrams (Calibration Curves), Expected Calibration Error (ECE), and Brier Score ($\frac{1}{N}\sum (\hat{p}_i - y_i)^2$).
  • Fixes: Temperature Scaling (dividing logits $z$ by a learned scalar $T > 1$ before softmax: $\text{softmax}(z/T)$), Platt Scaling (logistic fit), or Isotonic Regression.

Uncertainty Quantification

  • Aleatoric Uncertainty (Data Noise — Irreducible): Inherent randomness or overlapping classes/measurement noise in the data itself. Adding more training data cannot reduce it.
  • Epistemic Uncertainty (Knowledge Gap — Reducible): Uncertainty in model weights due to insufficient training data in a region of feature space (Out-of-Distribution / OOD). Adding more data reduces it. Estimated via MC Dropout (running dropout at inference time) or Deep Ensembles, or wrapped with Conformal Prediction for guaranteed coverage sets.

6. LLM & Generative AI Reliability (Hallucinations, Guardrails & Red Teaming)

  • Intrinsic vs. Extrinsic Hallucinations: Intrinsic hallucinations directly contradict the provided source context (e.g., in RAG); Extrinsic hallucinations fabricate unverifiable facts absent from the context.
  • Prompt Injection (Direct vs. Indirect): Direct jailbreaks come from the user explicitly trying to bypass system instructions; Indirect prompt injection hides malicious instructions inside retrieved external third-party documents, emails, or web pages ingested by a RAG/Agent pipeline.
  • Mitigation & Guardrails: Input/output safety classifiers (Llama Guard), strict system-data prompt separation, groundedness/faithfulness NLI verification checks, and automated AI Red Teaming prior to release.

Q1. A bank trains a credit approval model. Regulators require that among applicants who actually repay their loans ($Y=1$), both privileged ($A=1$) and unprivileged ($A=0$) groups must have the exact same approval rate. Which fairness metric does this describe?
Correct Answer: B (Equal Opportunity). Conditioning on $Y=1$ (actual positives) and requiring equal selection rates means equalizing the True Positive Rate (TPR / Recall) across groups: $P(\hat{Y}=1 \mid Y=1, A=0) = P(\hat{Y}=1 \mid Y=1, A=1)$. Equalized Odds would additionally require equal False Positive Rates ($Y=0$).
Q2. Under the EU AI Act, an AI system used by a company's HR department to automatically screen and rank job applicants' resumes falls into which regulatory tier?
Correct Answer: B (High Risk). Employment, recruitment, credit scoring, education admissions, and critical infrastructure are classified as High Risk under the EU AI Act, requiring strict conformity assessments, data governance, logging, and human oversight.
Q3. A hospital builds an algorithm to flag patients needing extra care. Because true "illness severity" is hard to measure directly, the team uses "historical annual medical billing cost" as the target label $Y$. Lower-income patients who had less access to hospitals are systematically under-flagged. Which bias is this?
Correct Answer: B (Measurement / Label Bias). Using a faulty proxy variable ("healthcare cost") to stand in for the true underlying target ("medical need") introduces systematic measurement bias into the labels. (This is the famous Obermeyer et al. healthcare algorithm study).
Q4. For a binary classification model where the dataset baseline expected log-odds prediction is $\phi_0 = 0.20$, an instance $x$ has three features with SHAP values $\phi_1 = +0.45$, $\phi_2 = -0.15$, and $\phi_3 = +0.10$. What is the model's exact prediction $f(x)$ for this instance?
Correct Answer: B (0.60). By SHAP's Local Accuracy (Additivity) axiom, $f(x) = \phi_0 + \sum_{i=1}^M \phi_i = 0.20 + 0.45 - 0.15 + 0.10 = 0.60$.
Q5. Why might a Partial Dependence Plot (PDP) show a completely flat horizontal line for feature $X_1$, even when $X_1$ strongly influences the model's predictions?
Correct Answer: B. Because a PDP plots the average marginal effect across all instances, opposing subgroup effects (e.g., $+1$ when $X_2 > 0$ and $-1$ when $X_2 < 0$) cancel out to zero. Plotting ICE (Individual Conditional Expectation) curves immediately exposes this X-shaped heterogeneity.
Q6. Which adversarial attack computes a single-step perturbation by taking the sign of the loss gradient with respect to the input image: $x_{\text{adv}} = x + \epsilon \cdot \text{sign}(\nabla_x J(\theta, x, y))$?
Correct Answer: A (FGSM). FGSM performs a fast, single-step gradient ascent step in input space bounded by $\ell_\infty$ norm $\epsilon$. PGD is its multi-step iterative counterpart.
Q7. An e-commerce recommendation model sees no change in user demographic profiles $P(X)$, but after a sudden macroeconomic inflation spike, users with the exact same profile stop buying luxury items, causing $P(Y \mid X)$ to change. What is this phenomenon called?
Correct Answer: B (Concept Drift). When the conditional mapping $P(Y \mid X)$ between features and target changes over time, it is Concept Drift. Covariate shift only changes $P(X)$ while $P(Y \mid X)$ stays constant.
Q8. A deep ResNet classifier achieves high top-1 accuracy, but its softmax confidence scores are severely overconfident (Expected Calibration Error is high). Which post-processing technique fixes calibration on a validation set using a single scalar parameter $T$ without changing the model's top-1 accuracy?
Correct Answer: B (Temperature Scaling). Dividing pre-softmax logits $z$ by a scalar $T > 1$ softens the probability distribution to match true empirical accuracy while preserving the relative ordering of logits (leaving argmax top-1 accuracy unchanged).
